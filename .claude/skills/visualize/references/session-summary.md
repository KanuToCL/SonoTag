# Session Summary

A compact, timeline-driven format for recapping work sessions, meetings, or daily progress. Action-oriented, scannable, structured.

---

## When to Use

Detect this archetype when:
- User mentions "summary", "recap", "worklog", "meeting notes", or "session"
- Content describes what happened during a period of time (session, day, week)
- There are decisions made, action items, or progress updates
- User asks to "summarize" a conversation, session, or work period
- Content has a natural chronological ordering of events or topics
- Output needs to be a working reference, not a polished presentation

---

## Communication Goal

A session summary communicates temporal narrative compressed into scannable structure. The reader should absorb what happened, what was decided, and what needs to happen next in under 30 seconds. Decisions and action items are the highest-value content and must never be buried in prose.

---

## Layout DNA

The page is a single vertical column with high information density and compact spacing. A header block anchors the top with session metadata (title, date, participants), followed immediately by 2-3 key outcome bullets that answer "what was accomplished?" at a glance. The timeline forms the structural spine of the page, running vertically through the middle section with chronological entries. Decisions get their own visually distinct callout blocks that break the timeline rhythm and demand attention. Action items collect at the bottom in a checklist format with owners and deadlines, acting as the "takeaway" section the reader returns to after the first scan.

---

## Interaction DNA

The page is mostly static and scannable, optimized for quick reading rather than interaction. Collapsible sections allow detailed notes and extended context to hide behind summary headers, keeping the default view tight. Checkboxes on action items provide satisfying tactile feedback when marking tasks complete. The reader's natural flow is top-to-bottom on first visit, then jumping directly to the action items section on return visits.

---

## Flavor Seeds

**Field Notebook** — Think of a geologist's leather-bound field journal: compact dated entries in the margin, quick sketches of key observations, starred items circled twice for emphasis. Warm paper tones, handwritten-style accents, personal and utilitarian at the same time.

**Mission Debrief** — A post-flight debrief board in a military operations room. Stark, grid-locked, monospace timestamps ticking down the left edge. Each decision stamped and filed. No decoration, pure signal. Dark background, phosphor-green text, everything classified by urgency.

**Film Dailies Sheet** — The producer's one-page summary after a day of shooting: scene numbers, takes completed, notes on what worked, what needs reshooting. Compact columns, handwritten marginalia, rubber-stamped approvals. Every line item earns its place on the page.

**Apothecary Ledger** — A Victorian pharmacist's daily log: precise entries with timestamps, ingredients measured and noted, outcomes recorded with clinical detachment. Sepia tones, ruled columns, elegant serif type, small red annotations for items requiring follow-up.

**Sprint Board Snapshot** — A photograph of a physical kanban board at end-of-day: sticky notes clustered in Done/In Progress/Blocked columns, handwritten task descriptions, colored dots for priority. The organic mess of real work, frozen in time.

---

## CDN Dependencies

None required. This archetype is purely structural and typographic.

---

## Anti-Patterns

- Don't write long paragraphs. Use bullets. This is a summary, not an essay.
- Don't omit action items. If nobody has a TODO, the session summary has failed its primary purpose.
- Don't present events without timestamps or chronological ordering, because temporal context is what separates a summary from random notes.
- Don't bury decisions inside narrative text. Decisions should be visually distinct and impossible to miss.
- Don't include everything that was discussed. Summarize ruthlessly and link to detailed notes if needed.
- Don't skip participant attribution. Action items without owners are wishes, not commitments.
- Don't use equal visual weight for all sections. The hierarchy is: decisions and action items first, timeline second, background notes last.
